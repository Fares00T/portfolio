Product case exercise, 2026

Reducing false payment declines

A payment gateway started declining customers who had done nothing wrong. How I would find the cause, fix it without weakening fraud protection, and prove it worked.

  1. SignalFraud flags up for 6 weeks, most of them legitimate
  2. CauseHosted Checkout makes customers retry like fraudsters
  3. FixClear reasons, controlled retries, 3-D Secure on the 3rd try
  4. ProofGated rollout, 5% to 100%, fraud held flat
My role
Product Manager
Format
Written brief, 24-slide deck
Domain
Payments, fraud, checkout
Type
Case exercise

Problem

Since Hosted Checkout became the default, banks flag many more payments as fraud. Most turn out to be real customers.

My approach

Validate the cause first, mitigate for the worst-hit merchants now, then fix the recovery flow the banks react to.

Recommendation

Fix the retry, not the bank

We can't change how banks score a payment. We can change what our checkout sends them.

The situation

Merchants use our online payment gateway to take payments on their websites. Over the past six weeks the monitoring dashboard has shown a sharp rise in transactions classified as fraudulent. Merchants and support report the same thing from two sides: customers can't complete their payments, and merchants see lower checkout conversion and lost sales.

Initial finding

A large share of the transactions flagged as fraud were later confirmed to be legitimate purchases.

That hurts three groups. Customers get declined, then retry or abandon. Merchants lose sales and trust in the gateway. And the business loses conversion while our fraud risk score with partner banks and our processing fees go up. So the goal is not just fewer declines: reduce false declines while keeping fraud protection strong.

What changed

Six weeks ago, Hosted Checkout became the default. Before, merchants built their own checkout and called our API. Now customers pay on a page we host.

Before

  1. Customer
  2. Merchant checkout
  3. Payment gateway API
  4. Bank / fraud system

6 weeks ago

  1. Customer
  2. Our Hosted Checkout
  3. Bank / fraud system

We now own the payment experience and the recovery UX. The bank still owns the fraud decision.

What the evidence actually says

  • Fraud classification is done by external partner bank systems, not by us.
  • Banks treat many payment attempts in a short window as suspicious.
  • Banks treat repeated card-detail changes in one checkout session as suspicious.
  • Hosted Checkout became the default around the time the problem appeared.
Correlation is not causation

The timing points at Hosted Checkout, but timing alone is not proof. I would validate the link before committing to a solution.

Four explanations, one to chase first

I ranked them by two things: how well the case supports them, and how much is within our control.

  • A
    Our checkout changed how people payChase firstIt went live when the problem started, and the behaviour it creates is exactly what the banks act on.
  • B
    The information we send the bank changedThe hosted page may send different device, address or merchant details. Banks score on those.
  • C
    The bank changed its rulesPossible, but we can't see their model. We would have to ask them.
  • D
    Different customers or merchantsPossible, and cheap to check: split the data and see if the problem sits in one group.

Inside hypothesis A, four things in the flow could each produce one of the two behaviours banks penalise, more attempts in a short window or changed card details in one session:

  • The error message doesn't say what went wrong, so the customer guesses and tries again.
  • The form clears after a decline, so they retype everything, which the bank reads as changed card details.
  • Nothing stops a double-click, so one click can send two attempts.
  • The session times out mid-payment, so they start over.

These are candidates, not findings. Each one is something I would check in the flow during validation.

Validation decision

If the data, user behaviour and stakeholder feedback all point to Hosted Checkout and repeated attempts, proceed with the recovery fix. If not, go back to B, C and D.

What I'm assuming

What we know

Banks say too many attempts in a short time looks suspicious. They don't say how many.

What I assume

4 attempts in 5 to 10 minutes

My number, not theirs. The first thing I'd check against our data and with the banks.

What we do

Slow the customer down on the 3rd attempt, before the line is reached. Ship it as a configurable policy, not a hard-coded number.

I also assume Hosted Checkout today shows no clear decline reason and has no 3-D Secure step-up on a retry. Validation answers that in a day. If it already does one of them, that part of the fix drops and the retry controls carry the work.

The recovery flow, before and after

Current experience

  1. Payment declined
  2. Customer doesn't know what happened
  3. Retry
  4. Change card
  5. Retry again
  6. Repeated attempts + card changes
  7. Likely false decline

Proposed experience

  1. Payment declined
  2. Clear reason + next best actionRetry payment, or use another card
  3. New card details? Review them firstbefore submitting again
  4. Still failing after the 3rd attempt

My early read

If attempts per session hasn't moved in 48 hours, I don't wait for the false-decline number. The fix isn't doing its job.

Decisions and trade-offs

  • Balanced recovery, not strict limits or unlimited retriesStrict limits stop legitimate customers from recovering. Unlimited retries send more suspicious attempts to the bank. Controlled retries with clear guidance sit between the two.Verification and cooldowns are friction, and friction costs conversion. We accept it only on the tail of sessions already heading for a decline. A short wait beats a hard refusal.
  • 3-D Secure only from the 3rd attemptAsking the bank to confirm the customer gives a real customer a way through.Some customers drop at the challenge, some banks don't support it, and passing it doesn't override the bank's own fraud decline. So it fires late, we measure how many finish it, and I'd test it with partner banks first.
  • Move the worst-hit merchants back to API Checkout nowIt protects their conversion while the real fix is built.Moving merchants twice is disruptive. We do it only while the fix is being built and remove the fallback the day it's live.
  • Accept that the bank still decidesWe can fix our checkout and the bank can still say no.Part of the outcome is outside our control, which is why the early metrics are behavioural ones we do control.

What I watch after launch, and when

First 24 hoursIs anything broken?Payment errors, fraud alerts, payment success rate. If one goes wrong, I stop the rollout the same day.
First 7 daysDid behaviour change?Attempts per session and card-change rate. A week is enough to see retry behaviour move.
14 to 30 daysDid the banks change their answer?False decline rate and actual fraud, cut by bank, merchant, country and method. Chargebacks arrive late, so these take weeks.

If the results don't improve

Check these in order, so the cheap checks come first:

  1. Are the numbers right?Are we counting false declines the same way as before?
  2. Did the behaviour change?If retries and card changes didn't drop, the fix isn't working yet.
  3. What did the bank do?Retries can drop while declines stay flat. That gap is on the bank's side.
  4. Then decideKeep fixing the flow, roll it back, or go back to the other explanations.

What I own after release

  • Share the results with merchants, Risk, Data and leadership, including what didn't improve.
  • Go back to the banks with the better false-decline numbers and ask them to review our risk score and fees.
  • Turn off the workaround: remove the API Checkout fallback and temporary flags so they don't become permanent.
  • Change how we ship defaults. This started because a default went live without a test behind it. The next one gets one.

This is a product case exercise answering a written brief about a hypothetical payment gateway. It uses no real company data, and the bank chart is illustrative.
